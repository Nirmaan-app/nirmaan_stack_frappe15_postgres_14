0Revision v3 — updated understanding

Written 2026-08-06 after your review of the v2 build, then revised twice the same day as you answered. All thirteen questions are now decided. One consequence needs your informed consent before I build — it is the single item in tab F.

Sections 1–12 are now the record, not the plan

Everything after this section describes the v2 feature as it sits in the repo today. Its spine — “the payment branch never writes” — is the thing you reversed. I have left it in place unedited, because it is an accurate account of what the code currently does and I will need it when I rewrite. Read it as history.

Your ask, restated

Upload a sheet of transfers that have already gone out of the bank, and map each one to the record it settles. Money is recorded in three places — Project Payments, Project Expenses, Non Project Expenses — and the mapper is for approved records in all three.

v2 got one thing badly wrong. It decided the Project Payments half should only look, never write, on the reasoning that accountants already tick payments Paid by hand. That is the half you actually care about, and excluding it hollowed out the feature. It comes back.

The new spine, in one sentence

The import pays what someone has already approved. It never approves anything, and it never creates a Project Payment.

Four consequences fall straight out of that sentence:

  1. A bank row can settle a Project Payment, a Project Expense or a Non Project Expense — always the same final step, Approved → Paid.
  2. A bank row that matches a record still sitting at Requested or CEO Pending is not a fraud finding and not a failure. It is a queue problem: the money went out ahead of the paperwork. Say that plainly, point at the right approval screen, and offer to run the match again.
  3. “Create a new entry” is one of the four choices, and it can only ever be an expense. A Project Payment is born from a PO or SR request; the import must not mint one.
  4. Every settle is a per-row human confirmation. Nothing settles itself, ever.

Near zero — because today everyone ticks Paid the instant they send the money, so Approved lasts minutes. Under your ruling that is not a problem to fix. It is simply what the ledger looks like when nobody has used this tool yet. An accountant who chooses the bulk route holds a set of approved payments, sends them, uploads the statement, and ticks them all at once. The pool exists because they chose to create it.

Two things follow, and both are already in the design:

And there is real work here on day one

111 payments sit at CEO Pending, worth ₹88.8 lakh. Money routinely leaves the bank while records are stuck in that queue — exactly the case you asked me to surface and nudge. That status will not be rare; on real statements it is likely the second-largest group on the screen, and it needs no behaviour change from anyone to start paying off.

Where the safety now lives

v2 was safe because it never wrote. v3 cannot lean on that. It is safe instead because it only ever writes the last rung of a ladder somebody else already climbed. It cannot approve, it cannot create a payment, and it cannot move a single rupee without a confirmation on that specific row. If a future change lets the import approve something, that is the invariant breaking — not a feature.

The mismatch case book — two cases, not three

M1 The bank paid less than the record says

bank row ₹ 2,32,650 ref 620919871893 matched PAY-00066-003 ₹ 2,35,000 Paid note → “The bank paid ₹2,350 less than the payment total of ₹2,35,000 (1.00% of it). A deduction such as TDS would look like this.”

What really causes it: TDS withheld at source (the note computes the implied rate for exactly this reason), a bank charge taken out of the transfer, or a short payment.

M2 The bank paid more

bank row ₹ 3,10,000 ref 742905000271 matched PAY-00074-011 ₹ 2,80,000 Paid note → “The bank paid ₹30,000 MORE than the payment total of ₹2,80,000. More money left the account than any matched payment claims.”

What really causes it: one transfer covering several payments where only some exist in Nirmaan — the fan-out shape, 40 real transfers, ₹10.0M, the largest one transfer across seven payments — or a genuinely wrong match.

M3 “Reference mismatch” is deleted — you were right to question it

You asked: why would the system check a payment's stored reference at all, for one that is already paid? There is no good answer under v3, and that is the tell.

It made sense in v2, where the payment side was a reconciliation report and “your ledger's UTR field contains a PO number” was a legitimate finding to publish. In v3 the job is to settle approved payments. An already-Paid payment is simply Skipped — there is nothing to learn from its reference field and nothing anyone would do about it here.

Combined with your Q5b ruling, the reference field is now only ever written (into a blank, when settling), never compared. The whole comparison branch goes. Mismatched is about amounts, full stop.

What that also retires: finding B1 from the previous revision — “a blank stored reference counts as a mismatch” — is moot. The fix is no longer a condition to correct; the check it lived in does not survive. It disappears as part of the status rewrite rather than as a patch.

Where Mismatched can still fire, precisely

Follow the two keys through. The bank-reference key only finds payments that have a reference, which means Paid ones. The vendor + amount + date key requires an exact amount, so it can never produce an amount disagreement. Therefore:

Mismatched only ever attaches to already-Paid records whose amounts do not add up — which is the fan-out finding you kept in Q4. A settleable Approved payment always comes out as a clean Matched. That is a narrow category, and an honest one.

B3 TDS payments will not match — and that is the cost of deferring Q11

An accountant approves ₹1,00,000, withholds ₹2,000 TDS, and sends ₹98,000. The bank row says ₹98,000. The approved payment says ₹1,00,000. Exact-amount matching finds nothing, so the row comes out Unmatched and has to be handled by hand.

I measured how often this happens:

709 of 7,421 Paid payments carry TDS (9.6%, ₹6.29 cr) by month: 2025-08 14.8% 2026-02 16.7% 2025-11 7.5% 2026-04 15.3% 2025-12 11.2% 2026-06 3.2% 2026-01 14.2% 2026-07 2.7% real examples: PAY-00066-272 amount 1,00,000 tds 2,000 -> bank sent 98,000 PAY-00089-088 amount 7,15,757 tds 14,315 -> bank sent 7,01,442 PAY-00102-163 amount 41,400 tds 414 -> bank sent 40,986

There is no cheap workaround. The obvious one — match on amount − tds — does not work, because tds is only recorded at the moment of fulfilment. An approved, unpaid payment has a blank TDS field, so the arithmetic has nothing to subtract. Catching these genuinely requires the tolerance pass you deferred in Q11.

Consequence: roughly 1 in 30 payments recently, 1 in 8 over the past year, cannot be bulk-settled in this version. They fall out as Unmatched and go through the existing screen — which, per Q12, is unchanged and still there. This is the one item in tab F.

B4 The TDS box from Q6 has nothing to attach to yet

You approved an optional TDS box on the settle card. It was designed to rescue case M1 — but M1 only fires on already-Paid records, which are not settleable, and the approved-payment case never reaches the card at all (B3). The box would never appear.

It travels with Q11 to the next version, where the tolerance pass gives it something to do. Building it now would be a control nobody can ever see.

1 · The period is already captured — you just never see it

period_from and period_to already exist on Outflow Import Batch, and upload already fills them from the earliest and latest Added On in the sheet. So half of what you asked for is built. What is missing is that it happens after the sheet is committed, silently, with nothing shown to you.

2 · Upload becomes two steps, and accepts .xlsx

Parse → preview → confirm. The preview is where the detected period appears, in the form, before anything is written. Q10 the reader takes .xlsx as well as .csv, so the sheet format stops being something the accountant has to think about.

┌─ Statement preview ───────────────────────────────────────────┐ │ cashfree_payouts_05aug.xlsx · Cashfree │ │ │ │ Period detected 30-Jul-2026 → 05-Aug-2026 │ │ Transfers 43 (41 successful · 2 failed) │ │ Gross out ₹ 48,21,900 │ │ Bank charges ₹ 2,124 │ │ │ │ ⚠ 3 transfers were already imported in OFI-26-00007 │ │ They will be staged and skipped, not re-matched. │ │ ⚠ This period overlaps OFI-26-00007 (28-Jul → 02-Aug) │ │ │ │ [ Cancel ] [ Import 43 transfers ] │ └───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

Small change, not a rebuild: the parse already happens before any write, on purpose, so a malformed sheet leaves no trace. The preview simply stops at that point and asks. The one mechanical detail is that the browser re-posts the same file on confirm — a few kilobytes — rather than the server holding a parse in memory between two requests.

3 · The duplicate check narrows by period first

Today it is one indexed lookup of every transfer id in the sheet against every import row ever recorded. You asked it to first pick the eligible uploads. New order: select the batches whose period overlaps this sheet's period, then check transfer ids inside those only.

This narrowing is safe, and here is why

A period pre-filter could in principle miss a duplicate living in a batch with an odd recorded period. It cannot cause double-payment, because the real backstop is a database unique constraint on (transfer id, target doctype, target name). The same transfer physically cannot be recorded against the same payment or expense twice, whatever the pre-filter does. The duplicate check gives you a clear message and saves work — it is not the safety net. So narrowing it costs nothing that matters.

4 · Duplicate-heavy sheets — refuse at 100%, warn from 90%

Straight with you: this is the expensive half

Marking an expense Paid is a field write. Marking a payment Paid sets off a chain. v2 deleted the machinery for that chain deliberately. Your answers have retired three of the five items below; two remain and both are small.

E1 A payment save commits mid-flight live

Setting a payment to Paid fires update_parent_amount_paid, which recomputes the PO's amount_paid and calls frappe.db.commit() inside the save. The expense path relies on a savepoint per row so that row 17 failing leaves rows 1–16 intact — a commit in the middle of that loop destroys the isolation. Payments therefore have to be settled one at a time with a deliberate commit, or the hook suppressed for this path.

This one matters more now that bulk is the selling point: “confirm all 8” must not be able to leave four written and four not, with no record of which.

E2 The UTR guard and one-transfer-many-payments retired by Q4

The existing settle helper refuses a UTR that already sits on another payment, which would have thrown on the second payment of a fan-out group. You chose report-only for those rows, so the guard is never challenged and stays exactly as it is.

E3 Overwriting a stored reference retired by Q5b

The data had already made this nearly impossible: utr is only written at fulfilment, so every non-Paid payment in the database has a blank one — 0 of 2 Approved, 0 of 111 CEO Pending, 0 of 22 Requested. Settling writes into an empty field. You ruled the remaining edge case cannot happen and the check should go, which also removed the M3 branch in tab B. Nothing left here.

E4 TDS deferred with Q11

Approved in principle (Q6), but unreachable until the tolerance pass exists — see B3 and B4 in tab B. Travels to the next version.

E5 Side effects fire per row live

Each payment status change also triggers the CEO-Hold cashflow recompute, the PO payment-term sync and notification dispatch. Fine for one row; a bulk confirm of forty is a different animal. The expense path already suppresses the equivalent recompute with a flag; the payment path needs the same treatment, decided deliberately rather than inherited.

Where that leaves the bill

Only E1 and E5 survive, and both are about doing a bulk write safely rather than about whether to write at all. Known, located, small. Nothing here should change your mind.

The last one, decided

Q14 · Does “only match Approved” also switch off the already-Paid duplicate check? Option A

Taken literally, “only Approved” would exclude records already marked Paid — and those are the ones somebody ticked by hand before uploading, which under Q12 is normal, not rare.

It is also the only thing that keeps Mismatched reachable, and mismatched rows are required to be resolvable.

Informed consent on TDS — not a new question, a consequence

Deferring Q11 has a price I did not know when you decided it, and you should re-confirm now that it is measured. A payment with TDS will not match, because the bank sends amount − tds and the matcher compares on exact amount. It comes out Unmatched and goes through the existing screen by hand.

Measured: 709 of 7,421 Paid payments (9.6%) carry TDS — but the trend matters: ~15% for most of the past year, ~3% in June and July 2026. Full numbers and worked examples are in tab B, finding B3.

Option A — accept it, ship without. At recent rates roughly 1 in 30 payments falls out. The existing screen still handles them; per Q12 it is unchanged and always available.
Option B — add a narrow TDS-only pass now. Same vendor, same date window, bank amount between 80% and 100% of the payment amount. It could only ever produce a suggestion requiring the TDS box to confirm — never an automatic settle — so a weak key can never cause a wrong write.

My lean: A, ship without. Your Q11 call stands up against these numbers. 3% is a small tail, the fallback is a screen people already use every day, and Option B drags the deferred TDS box back in with it. If the recent 3% turns out to be a blip and it climbs back to 15%, Option B is a contained addition — it does not require rework of anything decided above.

What changes is the outcome vocabulary (rewritten, one branch deleted), the write path (grows a third target), the upload flow (preview step, .xlsx), and the review screen (replaced by three tabs). 1The shape of it

One idea explains almost every design decision in this feature: the two kinds of record a bank transfer can belong to behave oppositely.

BranchBehaviourWhy it is that way
Payments
READ-ONLY		 Match, classify, report. Never writes to Project Payments, PO Payment Terms, or a PO's amount_paid. Accountants already mark payments Paid by hand at transfer time. By the time the statement is uploaded the payment is already Paid with a UTR — there is nothing left to settle, only something to check.
Expenses
WRITES		 Marks an approved expense Paid, or records a brand-new expense already at Paid. Expenses genuinely sit at Approved waiting for money. Site rent, accommodation, utilities, sundries — spend with no PO behind it. This is the productive half of the feature.
The one-sentence version

Of the five stages below, only one mutates anything outside the import's own staging tables, and it can only ever touch an expense. The payment branch is a reporting instrument.

The five stages

Stage A
Upload & stage
Parse the CSV in memory, then write a batch and one row per transfer. Auto-skips the obvious.
Stage B
Match
Three passes over payments and expenses. Proposes; never decides. Re-runnable all day.
Stage C
Settle
The only write. Reviewer settles an approved expense, or records a new one already Paid.
Stage D
Report
Findings, plus the reverse view — payments with no transfer behind them.
Stage E
Close
Bookkeeping, not a freeze. Records that a batch was abandoned with work outstanding.

Green top edge = reads only. Red = the write path.

The three staging doctypes

DoctypeNamingHolds
Outflow Import Batch OFI-.YY.-.##### One upload. The CSV attachment, the period, gross & charge totals, seven derived counters, the close record.
Outflow Import Row OFR-.YY.-.###### One transfer, verbatim — every raw column preserved — plus derived identity forms and the decision (row_status, skip_reason, outcome_note, decided_by/decided_at).
Outflow Row Match OFM-.YY.-.###### One (row → target) link. Carries a DB unique constraint on (transfer_id, target_doctype, target_name) — this, and not the UTR, is the idempotency guarantee.

A row is a separate linked doctype rather than a child table for a decisive reason: its matches would then be grandchildren, and Frappe never hydrates those.

3Stage A — Upload & stage

Endpoint upload.upload_outflow_statement. Synchronous — one request, one transaction. No worker, no Redis marker, no socket: a statement is a few kilobytes and ~50 rows.

  1. Authorize

    require_outflow_access(). Returns the acting user, who becomes uploaded_by.

  2. Validate the source

    Must be a source the parser has an adapter for. Today that is Cashfree only. The UI offers Cashbook (coming soon) as a disabled option; the server rejects it outright.

  3. Validate the file, and read the stream exactly once

    Extension must be .csv; size must be under 5 MB. The werkzeug stream is consumed on read — reading it twice yields nothing, so the bytes are captured once and reused.

  4. Parse — before any write whatsoever

    This ordering is the design. A malformed statement leaves no File, no batch, no rows. It matters here specifically because save_file is not rollback-able: the cloud-attachment hook uploads, rewrites file_url, deletes the local copy and calls frappe.db.commit() inside this request. Anything written before that point is committed mid-flight.

  5. Save the file, create the batch, create one row per transfer

    The CSV is stored once, private, attached to the batch. Each row is written verbatim — every original column preserved — plus two derived identity forms (normalized_account, normalized_reference).

  6. Decide each row's staged outcome

    Before any matching has happened. Only facts knowable without a match are decided here — see the ladder just below.

  7. Roll up and commit

    Counters and batch status are derived by status.py and written to the batch; one commit ends the request.

Whole-file rejections — nothing is written

Required columns are only those without which a row cannot be identified, matched or reported: Transfer Id, Added On, Amount, Status, Beneficiary Name, Bank Reference No. Unknown extra columns are ignored happily, and column order does not matter. Beneficiary Id is deliberately not required — it is stored nowhere in this database, so it can never resolve to anything; it is kept on the row for provenance only.

Pending
The parser never filters

A FAILED transfer is parsed and staged like any other, carrying its real status, and then visibly skipped. The reason is concrete: a failed transfer still carries a bank reference, and its successful retry appears as a separate row in the same file. Silently dropping the failure would leave nobody able to see why the numbers moved.

The two batch totals, and why they differ

gross_amount
Sums successful rows only. A failed transfer's beneficiary amount never left the account.
charges_amount
Sums gateway charge + tax across every row, failed ones included. A charge is money the bank took whatever the outcome, so excluding failures would understate the debit.

This asymmetry is also why the batch total can never equal the sum of its settled targets: the charges belong to no settlement target at all. Booking them is S7, not yet built — see §12.

Period overlap — a warning, never a block

If an earlier batch's period overlaps this one, the summary says so and links to it. That is all it does. Two exports can carry the same transfer with periods that do not overlap at all, and carry entirely different transfers with periods that do — so overlap is a hint, and the per-transfer-id check above is the actual guard.

Re-uploading the same statement is safe and legible

Every transfer is caught by rung 1, every row lands Skipped, no open rows remain, and the batch immediately reads Completed. Nothing is double-counted, and the duplicate batch is obvious rather than hidden.

4Stage B — Match

Endpoint review.match_batch, driven by the Run match button. Reads payments and expenses; writes only to the import's own staging rows.

Re-running is expected, not exceptional

Payments get marked Paid by hand throughout the day, so a batch matched at 10:00 finds more at 16:00. Each run rebuilds a row's matches from scratch — it deletes the row's existing match records before writing fresh ones, which is also what keeps the unique constraint from tripping on the second run. A re-run that finds fewer targets leaves no phantoms behind.

Two states are never re-matched

  • Skipped — a duplicate, a failed transfer, or a person's deliberate decision. Re-matching would silently overturn all three.
  • Settled — an expense was written against this row. Re-matching would strand that audit trail.

Step 1 — Resolve the vendor (proposes, never decides)

Two signals, scored, and an account hit deliberately does not short-circuit the name pass — when an account is shared, the name is the only thing that can order the tied candidates.

SignalScoreNotes

Shared accounts are legally distinct entities. Account 50200051045430 maps to three D.S. Ductofab companies with three different GSTs; 0000786000 to two Siemens entities. Nothing in a bank statement separates them. So an ambiguous resolution is left blank on the row — storing the top-ranked guess would turn a suggestion nobody chose into a recorded fact. resolved_vendor is written only when exactly one candidate survives.

Step 2 — Pass A: the bank reference

Key
normalize_reference(bank_reference_no) == normalize_reference(utr), both whitespace-stripped and upper-cased, on both sides.
Grouping
Every payment sharing the reference forms one group. This is the only pass that can discover fan-out.
Status filter
None, deliberately. A payment is returned whatever state it is in — finding a transfer against a not-Paid payment is one of the outcomes this feature exists to report. Filtering to Paid here would convert that finding into “no record found”, the opposite of the truth.
Short-circuit
If Pass A finds anything, Pass B does not run.

Why both sides must be normalised: 226 live Project Payments.utr values are whitespace-padded (e.g. ' 504918114686'). The existing duplicate guard elsewhere in the app strips the incoming value but compares it against unstripped storage, so those rows are invisible to it. The comparison is pushed into SQL as upper(btrim(utr)) so it matches exactly what the pure normaliser computes.

Step 3 — Pass B: vendor + exact amount + date window

Needed because 932 of 7,420 Paid payments (12.6%) carry a utr that is not a bank reference at all — PO numbers, short numbers, the literal string refund. Pass A cannot see those, and without Pass B they would read as unrecorded money.

  • Requires a resolved vendor. With no vendor it would degrade to “amount + date”, which on a 7,000-payment ledger matches far too much to put in front of a reviewer.
  • Amount must match exactly; date within ±3 days (an undated row or target cannot be excluded on evidence we do not have, so it passes).
  • A target with no vendor of its own is rejected — “matches because it has no vendor to disagree with” is the wrong reason to offer anything.
  • Single target only. A fan-out group's members each hold a fraction of the bank amount, so an exact-amount pass can never reassemble one. Rather than guess at a partition — which would mean inventing an allocation nobody authorised — an unreferenced fan-out stays Unmatched and says so.

Step 4 — Pass C: approved expenses

Exact amount, corroborated by the free-text description. Vendor cannot filter here and that is a property of the data, not an omission: Project Expenses.vendor is populated on 0.58% of rows and Non Project Expenses has no vendor column at all.

SignalAdds
Amount matches exactly (the base — never stands alone as a match, only as a candidate)0.40
Account number appears in the description (matched over the description's digits, so separators and leading zeros still hit)+0.40
Payee name words appear in the description+0.20

The pool is Project Expenses at Approved, and Non Project Expenses at Approved or Requested — the non-project side has no separate approval step in practice, so an Approved-only pool would be empty. Expense candidates are always a suggestion, never applied.

Step 5 — Persist

Per row: the derived status and note, the unambiguous vendor if there is one, then a fresh Outflow Row Match per matched target with match_kind = "Reconciled" (nothing on the target was mutated) and the basis that found it. Then the batch rollup, then one commit.

5The outcome ladder

status.derive_row_outcome is the single deriver — no endpoint, no controller, no frontend computes a status. It is checked strictly top to bottom, and the order is a series of deliberate judgements, not alphabetical accident.

1
Already handled in an earlier batch Not re-decided, whatever it would otherwise match. Offering it again is how the same money gets recorded twice.
Skipped
2
The transfer did not succeed Must come before any matching is considered: a FAILED transfer carries a real bank reference and would match a payment perfectly well.
Skipped
3
No payment group found The note distinguishes “no payment, but N approved expenses match this amount” from “nothing found at all”.
Unmatched
4
Any matched payment is not Paid Outranks every other matched outcome. “Money left the bank against a payment nobody had approved yet” is a finding about the organisation, not about this file, and it must not be masked by an amount delta that happens to sit on the same row.
Control exception
5
Group total ≠ bank amount Only reachable from Pass A, where the match was on the reference, so a delta is real. The note states the difference and its implied rate, and names TDS as the shape it would take — while stating plainly that nothing is written.
Amount mismatch
6
Matched on vendor+amount+date, but the payment's stored reference is a different one Only reachable from Pass B. The note names both the stored value and the real bank reference.
Reference mismatch
7
Several payments, amounts agree Fan-out. The note lists every payment in the group.
Reconciled
8
One payment, amount agrees The ordinary happy path.
Reconciled
Rungs 5 and 6 are mutually exclusive by construction, not by precedence

Pass A matched on the reference, so it can produce an amount delta but never a reference disagreement. Pass B matched on an exact amount, so it can produce a reference disagreement but an amount delta there is arithmetically impossible. Their order in the ladder never actually arbitrates anything — which is worth knowing before anyone “fixes” it.

How you get here

How you get here

Reviewer can
Nothing further. The row is frozen against both re-matching and skipping — re-matching would strand the audit trail, and skipping a row that already moved money would be a lie.

The expense; one Outflow Row Match with match_kind = "Settled", match_basis = "Manual"; the row's status, decided_by and decided_at.

Skipped terminal

Deliberately out of scope — automatically or by a person.

Three automatic routes
  • “Already imported in batch OFI-…”
  • “This transfer appears earlier in the same statement.”
  • “Transfer did not succeed at the bank (FAILED).”
One manual route
The Skip button, which requires a typed reason (owner ruling). Automatic skips carry a system-generated reason instead — making someone type “duplicate” forty times is theatre, not a control.
On screen
Muted chip; the reason is shown when the row is expanded. Candidates are not even fetched for a skipped row — there is no decision left to make, so the round trip is skipped too.
Reviewer can
Nothing. This is a one-way door — see Finding 2 in §12.
Writes
The row's status, reason, decided_by/decided_at; any existing match records for the row are deleted.

Error defined · currently unreachable

Meant for “a write failed and was rolled back”. Nothing in the shipped code ever assigns it.

Design intent
A per-row savepoint failure during a bulk settlement would mark that row Error and leave its neighbours intact.
As built
Settlement is one row per reviewer click. A failure rolls back to its savepoint, re-raises, and the named error is shown inline in the panel; the row keeps the status it had. So the status is declared, counted (error_rows), filtered, toned red — and never produced.
Consequence
Harmless today. It becomes load-bearing the moment a settle-everything action ships. Flagged as Finding 1 in §12.

7Stage C — The two writes

The only place in the feature where anything outside the import's own doctypes changes. Both paths write an expense and nothing else. Neither can reach a payment — settle.py has no code path to one.

Why these endpoints exist at all

There is no whitelisted expense endpoint anywhere else in this app. Every expense write today is a raw SDK updateDoc, which re-reads the doc server-side — so check_if_latest can never fire and there is zero optimistic-concurrency protection. Two accountants working overlapping statements would both succeed, and the second would silently overwrite the first's payment reference with no Version row to show it (both expense doctypes had track_changes unset until this feature turned it on). The row-locked status re-assertion below is the whole reason settlement is routed through here.

Path 1 — Settle an existing approved expense

expenses.settle_expense(row, target_doctype, target_name)

  1. Authorize

    The acting user becomes payment_by on the project side.

  2. Load the import row, and refuse the frozen ones

    Already Settled → “This row has already settled an expense.” Skipped → refused (see Finding 2).

  3. Open a savepoint

    So a failure rolls back this row only and leaves the database exactly as it was.

  4. Re-read the target under a row lock and re-assert everything the reviewer saw

    The reviewer's screen is a snapshot; between it and this call another accountant may have settled the same expense. Four named refusals, so the caller can tell the cases apart without matching on message text:

    ConditionErrorMessage
    Target not foundWrongStatusError“… not found.”
    Already PaidAlreadyPaidError“… was already marked Paid. Refresh to see who settled it.”
    Not a settleable statusWrongStatusError“… is 'Rejected' and cannot be settled from a bank statement.”
    Amount ≠ bank amountAmountMismatchError“… is for 5000 but 5500 left the bank. Settle it against the transfer that matches, or record a new expense.”
  5. Write the expense

    status → Paid, payment_date → the transfer date, payment_ref → the bank reference, and on the project side payment_by → the finalising user. Deliberately not the statement's “Added by”, which the gateway truncates to 15 characters.

  6. Record the import side

    The match record is written before the row flips, so a unique-constraint violation aborts the settlement rather than leaving a Settled row with nothing behind it.

  7. Release, roll up, commit

    One transaction, one commit. The four facts — expense written, match recorded, row flipped, batch rolled up — must not half-happen.

The lock has one fragile detail

The re-read uses for_update=True and must never carry cache=True. Frappe silently skips the row lock when the value comes from cache, so a cached read would take no lock at all and this entire guard would be decorative.

8Stage D — The reconciliation report

Second tab on the batch screen. Two lists that answer different questions; neither substitutes for the other.

List 1 — Reported findings

Every row in one of the three exception states, with its full note. Headed by a plain statement that this import never edits a payment, so each line is a description of something to look at, not a change that was made. When empty: “Nothing flagged. Every matched transfer agreed with the payment behind it.”

List 2 — Payments in this period with no transfer in the statement

The reverse view, and the reason the report exists. Matching a bank row to a payment answers “is this transfer recorded?”. It cannot answer “is every payment we recorded backed by a real transfer?” — and that second question is the one nobody asks until an audit does.

Population
Every Project Payments at Paid whose payment_date falls inside the batch period, minus those this batch matched.
Shown
Payment (linked), vendor name, amount, paid-on date, and the recorded reference — which is where a junk or padded UTR becomes visible.
Framing
Explicitly “informational, not an alarm — a payment may legitimately have gone out through another channel.”
Expect this list to be long on a first run

It includes every payment made through any other channel in the period, plus every payment whose stored reference is not a bank reference at all. That is the pre-existing data condition this report surfaces for the first time — 226 padded UTRs and 932 non-reference UTRs live in the ledger today. The owner has ruled this feature reports, never fixes.

Closing

Close import opens a confirmation that first calls get_close_preview, so it can say what would be abandoned — how many rows, how much money, and which ones — rather than imply it. Confirming stamps closed_at, closed_by and an optional reason on the batch, then re-derives the status.

Closing does NOT convert open rows to Skipped — and this reverses the earlier design

A skip is a decision — which is exactly why a manual one requires a typed reason. Auto-skipping on close would manufacture decisions nobody made, replacing “never decided” with a fabricated “deliberately skipped” on every row. Worse, it would make the batch read as a plain Completed, indistinguishable from one where everything really was resolved. Instead the abandonment is recorded once, on the batch.

Closing is bookkeeping, not a freeze. An abandoned row can still be settled afterwards, and the status re-derives when it is. A banner on the batch says so in as many words: “Rows left undecided keep their status and can still be settled.” Reopen clears the three close fields — a mis-click must not be permanent.

10Worked examples, from real data

Every rule in the matcher was derived from an observed failure. These are the ones that shaped it.

The failed transfer and its retry

Sri Sai Roadlines · ₹22,000 · 28-Jul, in one 19-row statement.

FAILED at 18:17:59 with bank reference 620918791146; succeeded at 19:23 with 620919871893. Both would otherwise match the same payment. The parser stages both, the failed one is skipped at rung 3 with its real status named, and the retry reconciles. A parser that filtered failures out would have left nobody able to see why the numbers moved.

One transfer, seven payments, six projects

Reference 742905000271 · ₹7,289,432.

Fan-out is real: 40 such transfers cover 99 payments — 0.55% of bank rows, but 2.53% of settled value (₹10.0M). Shape: 31 pairs, then 3(×5), 4, 5, 6, and this 7. Pass A groups them by shared reference and compares the sum against the bank amount. No allocation, no split, no write. Drop the grouping and these 99 payments produce 40 matches and 59 spurious exceptions, concentrated on the largest transfers.

One bank account, three companies

Account 50200051045430 → three D.S. Ductofab entities, three GSTs.

The account narrows the candidate set to three; nothing in the statement separates them. The row shows all three with their reasons and an amber “More than one vendor matches” note, resolved_vendor stays blank, and a human picks. 0000786000 does the same for two Siemens entities.

The expense found only by its description

28-Jul · Ravindra reddy kuppagiri · ₹5,000 · a/c 39088842277.

No payment. One Approved Staff Accommodation Rent expense at exactly ₹5,000, whose description reads “Wasim Alam (July PG rent) / Account Number: 39088842277 / …”. Amount match scores 0.40, the account number in the description adds 0.40. The beneficiary name and the expense's own text share nothing — this match exists only because someone typed the account number into a free-text field. Real signal, fragile signal: it corroborates, it never stands alone, and it is always a suggestion.

11Invariants that break silently

Everything else can be re-derived by reading the code. These cannot — each one fails without an error, and four of the five were discovered the hard way.

  1. The payment branch must never write. No code reachable from this feature may touch Project Payments, PO Payment Terms, or a PO's amount_paid. Pinned by a test. settle.py has no code path to a payment at all — that is not an accident, and it is what makes the guarantee structural rather than a rule someone has to remember.

  2. Indexes need explicit names. PostgreSQL index names are unique per schema, Frappe generates them with no table prefix, and CREATE INDEX IF NOT EXISTS matches by name only — so a generic name colliding with another table makes the call a silent no-op. Every index here passes an explicit ofi_/ofr_/ofm_ name. (Also: never name a field user or order — both are PG reserved words.)

  3. for_update=True must never carry cache=True. Frappe skips the row lock when the value comes from cache, which would make the entire concurrency guard decorative.

  4. The two expense doctypes are not twins. See the table in §7 — a Data amount column, a payment_by that exists on one side only, and no vendor column at all on the other.

  5. Outflow Import Row.remarks must stay Text. As Data it is varchar(140) and Frappe throws CharacterLengthExceededError rather than truncating — the same trap the expense description field hit on 2026-07-28. A real statement carries a 201-character remark.

Two more worth knowing

  • The identity / scoring split. normalize_account and normalize_reference produce identity forms — two values that normalise equal are treated as the same thing. normalize_name and name_tokens produce scoring forms and are lossy on purpose (singularisation is a heuristic and would be indefensible as an identity rule). A normalised name must never become a key.
  • Money is Decimal end to end, converted to float only at the persistence boundary. These figures are compared for exact equality and then differenced; binary floating point makes both unreliable at the paisa level.

12Findings & your decisions

Four gaps found while tracing the code against the design, then the decision points the build deliberately left open, then what remains to build.

F2A skipped row is a one-way door

There is no un-skip. match_batch treats Skipped as frozen and never re-matches it, and no endpoint clears the status. But when a reviewer tries to settle one, the refusal reads: “This row was skipped. Re-run the match to reconsider it.” — an instruction that cannot work, because re-running the match is precisely what skips over it.

Consequence: an accountant who skips the wrong row has permanently removed that transfer from the feature. The money can still be recorded by hand elsewhere in the app, but not through this import, and the batch will carry a decision nobody can revise.

Your call: (a) add an Undo skip that returns a manually-skipped row to Pending — automatic skips staying frozen; (b) leave the behaviour and just fix the misleading message; or (c) leave both as-is because a skip is meant to be final.

F1The Error status can never occur

It is declared in both the Python and TypeScript vocabularies, counted as error_rows on every batch, offered in the status filter, and toned red — and no code path assigns it. Settlement is one row per click; a failure rolls back to its savepoint, re-raises, and shows a named message in the panel while the row keeps its previous status.

That is arguably the right behaviour for an interactive per-row write. But the design described a bulk settlement where row 17 fails, is marked Error, and rows 1–16 survive — so the vocabulary is sized for a feature that was not built.

Your call: keep Error reserved for a future bulk action (costs nothing, but a counter that is always zero is noise), or drop it from the vocabulary and the batch counters now.

F3resolved_project is read but never written

The field exists on Outflow Import Row, is selected by the review query, and is declared in the TypeScript type — nothing ever populates it. Harmless, but it reads as a feature that exists. (resolved_vendor beside it is populated, which makes the omission look deliberate when it is probably just unfinished.)

Your call: populate it from a matched payment's project, or remove it.

F4Draft never persists

The batch is created as Draft, then rolled up in the same request — and since the parser refuses a file with zero rows, the rollup always finds at least one status. So no batch ever comes to rest in Draft. Cosmetic; noted so nobody hunts for it.

Decision points the build left open, on purpose

D1Vendor scoring weights

The code marks these an owner decision point in as many words. The current values (account 0.80, account+IFSC 0.95, exact name 0.75, token weight 0.60, floor 0.35) encode two judgements only someone who works with this vendor master can make:

  • How much more does an account hit count than a name hit? Weight the account too hard and you bury the live case where a vendor was paid from an account the master does not hold at all, so the name was the only route. Too little, and the account-only matches stop standing out.
  • Where is the line between “pre-highlight” and “one of several”? Set the floor high and a genuine trailing-space / ampersand / plural variant drops out of the list entirely. Set it low and every “Sri Sai *” vendor appears on every Sri Sai row — and the reviewer stops reading the list.
Nothing auto-commits either way — these weights only change the order and length of a list a person confirms, which is what makes them safe to tune later against real reviewer feedback. Recommendation: leave them, and revisit after the first real statement is reviewed end to end.

D2N targets per bank row (R2) — taken as yes without explicit confirmation

Fan-out grouping was built on a recommendation, flagged in the plan as never explicitly confirmed. Reversing it is cheap — drop the grouping, take the single best candidate — and costs 59 spurious exceptions on the largest transfers.

Your call: confirm R2 stands, or reverse it.

D3Non-project expenses are settleable at Requested

The project side settles only from Approved. The non-project side also accepts Requested, on the reasoning that it has no separate approval step in practice and an Approved-only pool would be empty. It does mean this feature can mark something Paid that was never approved by anyone.

Your call: accept, or restrict the non-project side to Approved and accept a smaller candidate pool.

D4The ±3-day window

A payment is recorded the same day 98.9% of the time; three days covers a Friday transfer recorded on Monday without reaching into the next week's run. Widening it grows the Pass B suggestion list.

Your call: confirm 3 days, or name another number.

What remains to build

S7Bank charges — opt-in booking

Every batch already carries charges_amount (gateway charge + tax across every row, failed ones included) and displays it. It is not booked. S7 makes booking it one click: create one Non Project Expenses of type Bank Fees & Charges at Paid, dated period_to, and store it in the charges_expense link that already exists on the batch. Guarded against double-booking and against a zero charge.

Owner ruling already on record: opt-in, never automatic — a batch that never books its charges is a valid batch. Confirm that still holds and this is worth building now.

S8Documentation

A domain doc with a residence manifest, a row in the root reference table, the load-bearing invariants added to the always-loaded conventions, and the as-built record appended to the plan.

—Not scheduled, worth knowing

  • Cashbook is listed-but-unavailable. Adding it is one adapter entry (a column map plus its required set) and enabling it in the source picker. No other module is source-aware.
  • The Miscellaneous Expenses expense type (non_project = 1) you ruled for Diesel/Garbage bills has not been created — it is master data, to be added in Desk.
